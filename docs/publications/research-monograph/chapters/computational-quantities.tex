\section{Learning goals}

After working through this chapter, a reader should be able to

\begin{enumerate}
  \item distinguish a mathematical number from a computational quantity;
  \item attach units, coordinate meaning, indexing conventions, and provenance
        to numerical values;
  \item explain why shape and data type do not determine scientific meaning;
  \item identify when an operation requires an explicit unit conversion,
        coordinate transformation, or reference alignment; and
  \item identify defining metadata that must accompany a retained array.
\end{enumerate}

This monograph begins with data rather than with a Hamiltonian. Every later
vector, matrix, grid, and operator is stored as numbers, and those numbers are
usable only when their interpretation is explicit.

\section{From numbers to quantities}
\index{number!quantity distinction}

A floating-point value such as \(5.43\) is a number. It becomes a computational
quantity only after its role and unit are supplied. A lattice length might be
recorded schematically as
\begin{equation}
  q=(5.43,\ \text{angstrom}).
\end{equation}
A conversion changes the numerical value and the unit together while preserving
the physical quantity. Changing only one member of the pair changes the
meaning.

The same rule applies to arrays. An array does not announce whether it contains
positions, reciprocal vectors, energies, expansion coefficients, or matrix
elements. Its shape and data type are necessary software facts, but they are not
a scientific interpretation.

\begin{definition}[Computational quantity]
A computational quantity is a numerical value or array together with the
metadata required to interpret it for a declared operation. Depending on the
operation, that metadata can include units, coordinate system, axis meanings,
ordering, normalization, reference value, geometry, and provenance.
\end{definition}

The phrase ``required to interpret'' is operation dependent. Adding two
energies requires compatible energy units and references. Taking a dot product
of Cartesian displacement vectors requires compatible coordinates and length
units. Comparing represented Hamiltonians requires substantially more, as later
chapters show.

\index{notation!numeric type and shape}

\subsection{Number sets and scalar notation}

We begin with the standard number sets. To avoid the common ambiguity over
whether zero is a natural number, we write
\begin{subequations}
\label{eq:training-natural-number-conventions}
\begin{align}
  \mathbb N_0
  &=\{0,1,2,\ldots\},
  \label{eq:training-natural-numbers-with-zero}\\
  \mathbb N_{>0}
  &=\{1,2,3,\ldots\}.
  \label{eq:training-positive-natural-numbers}
\end{align}
\end{subequations}
The integers are
\begin{equation}
  \mathbb Z=\{\ldots,-2,-1,0,1,2,\ldots\}.
\end{equation}
We write \(\mathbb R\) for the real numbers. Let \(\mathrm i\) denote the
imaginary unit, with \(\mathrm i^2=-1\). The complex numbers are then
\begin{equation}
  \mathbb C
  =\left\{a+b\,\mathrm i\;\middle|\;a,b\in\mathbb R\right\}.
  \label{eq:training-standard-number-sets}
\end{equation}
The four sets satisfy the familiar inclusions
\begin{equation}
  \mathbb N_0\subseteq\mathbb Z\subseteq\mathbb R\subseteq\mathbb C.
\end{equation}
Finite dimensions and nonzero counts are taken from \(\mathbb N_{>0}\), so
\begin{equation}
  d,m,n,N\in\mathbb N_{>0}.
\end{equation}
Integer indices and lattice coordinates take values in \(\mathbb Z\), while
real coordinates and complex amplitudes take values in \(\mathbb R\) and
\(\mathbb C\), respectively.

Throughout this chapter, italic lowercase letters denote scalars,
\begin{equation}
  x\in\mathbb R,
  \qquad
  z\in\mathbb C.
\end{equation}

\section{Vectors}
\index{vector!coordinate notation}

Bold lowercase letters denote finite coordinate column vectors. We first write
a real vector as
\begin{subequations}
\label{eq:training-basic-vector-notation}
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf x
  =\begin{bmatrix}
      x_1\\
      \vdots\\
      x_d
    \end{bmatrix}
  =\begin{bmatrix}x_1&\cdots&x_d\end{bmatrix}^{\mathsf T}
  \in\mathbb R^d.
  \label{eq:training-basic-real-vector-notation}
\end{equation}
Here \(\mathbf x\) names the vector, and \(x_j\in\mathbb R\) is its
\(j\)-th component for \(j=1,\ldots,d\). The bracketed expression
\(\begin{bmatrix}x_1&\cdots&x_d\end{bmatrix}\) is a \(1\times d\) row. The
superscript \(\mathsf T\) transposes it into the displayed \(d\times1\)
column. The set
\begin{equation*}
  \mathbb R^d
  =\underbrace{\mathbb R\times\cdots\times\mathbb R}_{d\ \text{factors}}
\end{equation*}
contains all such ordered real coordinate columns. The superscript \(d\)
records the number of components; it is not an exponent applied to a particular
real number.

The same coordinate notation works with other underlying number sets. Integer
coordinate columns and complex coordinate columns are written, for example, as
\begin{align}
  \mathbf n
  &=\begin{bmatrix}n_1&\cdots&n_d\end{bmatrix}^{\mathsf T}
    \in\mathbb Z^d,
  \qquad n_j\in\mathbb Z,
  \label{eq:training-basic-integer-vector-notation}\\
  \mathbf z
  &=\begin{bmatrix}z_1&\cdots&z_d\end{bmatrix}^{\mathsf T}
    \in\mathbb C^d,
  \qquad z_j\in\mathbb C.
  \label{eq:training-basic-complex-vector-notation}
\end{align}
\end{subequations}
Thus \(\mathbf x\), \(\mathbf n\), and \(\mathbf z\) have the same column
shape but different allowed component sets. Their membership declarations do
not yet identify the entries as positions, lattice coordinates, energies,
samples, or model parameters. They specify numerical structure, not scientific
interpretation.

This notation describes numerical structure, not physical meaning. If the
column \(\mathbf x_{u_L}\in\mathbb R^d\) gives the components of a physical
displacement \(\mathbf r\) in a length unit \(u_L\), we write
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf r=\mathbf x_{u_L}u_L.
  \label{eq:training-vector-unit-representation}
\end{equation}
Similarly, \(N\) energies reported relative to a reference \(E_0\) may be
written
\begin{equation}
  \overline{\mathbf E}^{[E_0]}
  =\mathbf e_{u_E}^{[E_0]}u_E,
  \qquad
  \mathbf e_{u_E}^{[E_0]}\in\mathbb R^N,
  \label{eq:training-energy-array-numeric-representation}
\end{equation}
where \(u_E\) is the energy unit. Membership in \(\mathbb R^N\) gives only
the numerical field and shape. The unit, reference, ordering, and physical role
come from the notation and the accompanying record. Chapter~\ref{ch:training-finite-representations}
will make the related distinction between an abstract vector and its coordinate
column.

\begin{definition}[Vector space]
\label{def:training-vector-space}
Let \(\mathbb F\) be either \(\mathbb R\) or \(\mathbb C\). A vector space
\(\mathcal V\) over \(\mathbb F\) is a set equipped with vector addition
\(\mathcal V\times\mathcal V\to\mathcal V\) and scalar multiplication
\(\mathbb F\times\mathcal V\to\mathcal V\). There is an additive identity
\(0_{\mathcal V}\in\mathcal V\), and every \(u\in\mathcal V\) has an
additive inverse \(-u\in\mathcal V\). For all \(u,v,w\in\mathcal V\) and
\(\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb F\), the operations satisfy
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
  u+v&=v+u,
  & (u+v)+w&=u+(v+w),\\
  u+0_{\mathcal V}&=u,
  & u+(-u)&=0_{\mathcal V},\\
  \alpha(u+v)&=\alpha u+\alpha v,
  & (\alpha+\beta)u&=\alpha u+\beta u,\\
  \alpha(\beta u)&=(\alpha\beta)u,
  & 1u&=u.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:training-vector-space-axioms}
\end{equation}
Here \(0_{\mathcal V}\) is the additive identity, and \(-u\) is the additive
inverse of \(u\). The choice of scalar field \(\mathbb F\) is part of the
vector-space definition.
\end{definition}

The coordinate spaces \(\mathbb R^d\) and \(\mathbb C^d\) are vector spaces
over \(\mathbb R\) and \(\mathbb C\), respectively, under componentwise
addition and scalar multiplication.

\subsection{Vector subspaces}
\index{vector subspace}

A subset of a vector space is not automatically a vector space under the
inherited operations. It must remain closed under the combinations allowed in
the ambient space.

\begin{definition}[Vector subspace]
\label{def:training-vector-subspace}
Let \(\mathcal V\) be a vector space over \(\mathbb F\). A subset
\(\mathcal S\subseteq\mathcal V\) is a vector subspace of \(\mathcal V\)
when
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \(0_{\mathcal V}\in\mathcal S\);
  \item \(u+v\in\mathcal S\) whenever \(u,v\in\mathcal S\); and
  \item \(\alpha u\in\mathcal S\) whenever
        \(\alpha\in\mathbb F\) and \(u\in\mathcal S\).
\end{enumerate}
Equivalently, \(\alpha u+\beta v\in\mathcal S\) for all
\(u,v\in\mathcal S\) and all \(\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb F\).
\end{definition}

For example,
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal S=\{\mathbf x\in\mathbb R^d\mid x_d=0\}
\end{equation}
is a vector subspace of \(\mathbb R^d\). Replacing the condition by
\(x_d=1\) produces an affine set rather than a vector subspace. Also,
\(\mathbb Z^d\) is a useful set of integer coordinate columns, but it is
not a vector space over \(\mathbb R\) or \(\mathbb C\), because arbitrary real
or complex scalar multiplication does not preserve integer entries.

\section{Matrices}
\index{matrix!transpose}
\index{matrix!conjugate transpose}
\index{Hermitian matrix}

Capital italic letters denote matrices and other two-index arrays. Their two
dimensions record the numbers of rows and columns.

\subsection{Real matrices}

A real matrix is a rectangular array of real numbers organized into \(m\)
rows and \(n\) columns. Written entry by entry,
\begin{equation}
  A
  =\begin{bmatrix}
      A_{11} & A_{12} & \cdots & A_{1n}\\
      A_{21} & A_{22} & \cdots & A_{2n}\\
      \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots\\
      A_{m1} & A_{m2} & \cdots & A_{mn}
    \end{bmatrix}
  \in\mathbb R^{m\times n}.
  \label{eq:training-basic-matrix-notation}
\end{equation}
The notation \(\mathbb R^{m\times n}\) denotes the set of all real matrices
with this shape. We abbreviate the displayed matrix as \(A=(A_{ij})\), where
\(A_{ij}\in\mathbb R\) is the entry in row \(i\) and column \(j\), with
\(1\leq i\leq m\) and \(1\leq j\leq n\).

\begin{definition}[Transpose of a matrix]
\label{def:training-matrix-transpose}
Let \(A=(A_{ij})\in\mathbb R^{m\times n}\). The transpose of \(A\) is the
matrix \(A^{\mathsf T}\in\mathbb R^{n\times m}\) defined by
\begin{equation}
  \left(A^{\mathsf T}\right)_{ji}=A_{ij},
  \qquad
  1\leq i\leq m,
  \quad
  1\leq j\leq n.
  \label{eq:training-matrix-transpose-definition}
\end{equation}
Thus row \(i\) of \(A\) becomes column \(i\) of \(A^{\mathsf T}\).
\end{definition}

The standard size-dependent symbols
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf 0_d,\mathbf 1_d\in\mathbb R^d,
  \qquad
  I_d\in\mathbb R^{d\times d}
\end{equation}
denote the zero column, the column of ones, and the identity matrix,
respectively.

A table of \(N\) samples, each with \(d\) components, is written with one
sample per row:
\begin{equation}
  X
  =\begin{bmatrix}
      \mathbf x_1^{\mathsf T}\\
      \vdots\\
      \mathbf x_N^{\mathsf T}
    \end{bmatrix}
  \in\mathbb R^{N\times d},
  \qquad
  \mathbf x_i\in\mathbb R^d.
  \label{eq:training-point-table-notation}
\end{equation}
The first index therefore labels the sample, and the second labels the
component. The transpose \(X^{\mathsf T}\in\mathbb R^{d\times N}\) can encode
the same collection only after the change of layout has been stated explicitly.

\subsection{Complex matrices}

A complex \(m\times n\) matrix is written
\begin{equation}
  A=(A_{ij})\in\mathbb C^{m\times n},
  \qquad
  A_{ij}\in\mathbb C.
  \label{eq:training-basic-complex-matrix-notation}
\end{equation}
The transpose uses the same exchange of row and column indices as in
Definition~\ref{def:training-matrix-transpose}. Complex matrices also carry a
second operation that conjugates the entries as it exchanges the indices.

\begin{definition}[Conjugate transpose of a matrix]
\label{def:training-matrix-conjugate-transpose}
For \(A\in\mathbb C^{m\times n}\), the conjugate transpose is
\begin{equation}
  A^{\dagger}=\overline A^{\mathsf T}\in\mathbb C^{n\times m},
  \qquad
  \left(A^{\dagger}\right)_{ji}=\overline{A_{ij}},
  \label{eq:training-matrix-conjugate-transpose-definition}
\end{equation}
where the overline denotes entrywise complex conjugation.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Hermitian matrix]
\label{def:training-hermitian-matrix}
A square complex matrix \(H\in\mathbb C^{N\times N}\) is \emph{Hermitian} when
\begin{equation}
  H=H^{\dagger}.
  \label{eq:training-hermitian-definition}
\end{equation}
Equivalently,
\begin{equation}
  H_{ij}=\overline{H_{ji}}
  \qquad
  \text{for all }i,j\in\{1,\ldots,N\}.
\end{equation}
A real square matrix is Hermitian exactly when it is symmetric:
\(H=H^{\mathsf T}\).
\end{definition}

\section{Indexing, axis meaning, and ordering}
\index{array ordering}
\index{indexing convention}

Computational arrays have both mathematical axes and storage conventions. For a
table \(X\in\mathbb R^{N\times d}\), rows might enumerate points and columns
might enumerate Cartesian components. Another program may store the transpose.
Both layouts can be valid, but an unlabeled shape does not distinguish them.

Index origins also matter. Mathematical derivations often number basis vectors
from \(1\) to \(N\), whereas Python arrays use indices from \(0\) to \(N-1\).
A translation between these conventions should occur at a named boundary, not
implicitly inside a scientific argument.

For tensor-product grids, a multi-index must also be mapped to a linear index.
C ordering makes the final axis vary fastest; Fortran ordering makes the first
axis vary fastest. Two matrices assembled with different flattening conventions
can have identical shapes and spectra while assigning entries to different
physical grid points.

\section{Metadata needed by represented scientific data}
\index{represented data}

The interpretation of a finite represented operator can depend on

\begin{itemize}
  \item the identity and dimension of the represented state space;
  \item basis identity, orbital convention, and ordering;
  \item geometry, lattice vectors, site labels, and boundary convention;
  \item units and normalization;
  \item scalar energy reference;
  \item spin representation;
  \item gauge, phase, or other coordinate conventions; and
  \item provenance linking the record to its inputs and construction.
\end{itemize}

Not every elementary array needs every field. The rule is instead that the
record must carry enough information for its promised operations to fail closed.
If subtraction requires a common energy reference, an absent reference is not
permission to assume one. If a decoder promises a column-vector convention, a
row-vector document is not an alternative spelling of the same record.

It is useful to distinguish defining data from derived data. A primitive basis
can define a metric, reciprocal basis, and projector. Serializing all of them as
independent authorities creates the possibility of internal disagreement.
Retaining the defining data and deterministically recomputing derived data often
produces a stronger record.

\section{A small transparent record}

The following example keeps an energy array, its unit, and its reference in one
visible record. It uses a plain dictionary only for pedagogy; maintained project
interfaces use typed immutable records and explicit validation.

\begin{pythonexample}{converting an energy quantity without losing its reference}
import numpy as np

# These synthetic levels are measured relative to one declared energy zero.
levels_ev = np.array([0.0, 0.12, 0.31], dtype=np.float64)
record_ev = {
    "values": levels_ev,
    "unit": "eV",
    "energy_reference": "illustrative band edge",
    "axis_meaning": "state index in ascending eigensystem order",
}

# Convert the values and unit together; preserve the non-numeric metadata.
ev_per_hartree = 27.211386245988
record_hartree = {
    **record_ev,
    "values": record_ev["values"] / ev_per_hartree,
    "unit": "hartree",
}

# Recover the original numerical representation as an independent check.
np.testing.assert_allclose(
    record_hartree["values"] * ev_per_hartree,
    record_ev["values"],
)
assert record_hartree["energy_reference"] == record_ev["energy_reference"]
\end{pythonexample}

The example verifies one conversion identity for synthetic values. It does not
establish a project serialization contract or any physical energy level.

\section{Compatibility before arithmetic}
\index{compatibility!before arithmetic}

A robust computational sequence is
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
  \text{decode}
  &\longrightarrow \text{validate each record}
  \longrightarrow \text{check pairwise compatibility}\\
  &\longrightarrow \text{perform arithmetic}
  \longrightarrow \text{retain the result context}.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
Validation of each operand and compatibility between operands answer different
questions. Two individually valid energy arrays can still use different units,
references, bases, or state orderings.

Automatic inference should be limited to conventions owned by a documented
contract. Inferring a unit from the magnitude of an array, an axis meaning from
its length, or a physical correspondence from matching labels replaces an
explicit scientific decision with a heuristic.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item List the metadata needed to add two Cartesian force vectors and explain
        which fields are unnecessary for that operation.
  \item Give two different scientific interpretations of a complex
        \(4\times4\) array that have the same shape and data type.
  \item Describe why two energy arrays with identical units but different
        scalar zeros cannot be subtracted as a physical perturbation without
        alignment.
  \item Write a small record for three fractional coordinates stored by rows.
        Include dimension, axis meaning, origin convention, and provenance.
  \item Classify the direct basis, Gram metric, and reciprocal basis as defining
        or derived data under the convention developed in
        Chapter~\ref{ch:training-periodic-geometry}.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Evidence boundary}

Metadata completeness is relative to a declared operation and contract. A
successful decode or round trip establishes bounded software behavior; it does
not prove that the recorded metadata are truthful, that the source calculation
was adequate, or that two physical systems should be compared. The next two
chapters add finite coordinate representations and then Hermitian eigensystems
while preserving this distinction between numbers and represented meaning.
