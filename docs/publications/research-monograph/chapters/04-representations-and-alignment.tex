\chapter{Representations, Localization, and Alignment}
\label{ch:representations-and-alignment}

\textbf{Status: technical framework and planned diagnostics; no accepted Wannier
operator or alignment result is claimed. Literature attribution remains to be
completed from verified primary sources.}

The Kohn--Sham parent is naturally described by Bloch states, whereas local
lattice models and substitutional perturbations are naturally discussed in
real space. Moving between these descriptions requires several operations that
must not be collapsed into the single word ``Wannierization.'' This chapter
separates subspace selection, projection, disentanglement, gauge selection,
localization, basis transformation, real-space truncation, and alignment.

\section{Bloch representation}

For a periodic parent problem, Bloch states may be written
\begin{equation}
  \psi_{n\mathbf k}(\mathbf r)
  =
  e^{i\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf r}u_{n\mathbf k}(\mathbf r),
\end{equation}
where $u_{n\mathbf k}$ has the periodicity of the crystal lattice, $n$ is a band
label, and $\mathbf k$ is a crystal momentum expressed in a declared reciprocal
coordinate convention. The eigenvalue relation at fixed $\mathbf k$ is
\begin{equation}
  \widehat H(\mathbf k)
  \lvert\psi_{n\mathbf k}\rangle
  =
  \varepsilon_{n\mathbf k}
  \lvert\psi_{n\mathbf k}\rangle.
\end{equation}
Band labels alone do not provide a globally smooth frame. Degeneracies,
crossings, and entanglement with excluded bands make the retained subspace, not
an informal list of band indices, the stable object to identify.

\section{Target subspace}

At each sampled $\mathbf k$, let $\mathcal S(\mathbf k)$ be an
$M$-dimensional retained subspace with orthogonal projector
\begin{equation}
  P(\mathbf k)
  =
  \sum_{m=1}^{M}
  \lvert\psi_{m\mathbf k}\rangle
  \langle\psi_{m\mathbf k}\rvert
\end{equation}
for any orthonormal frame spanning that subspace. The immediate bulk target must
contain the valence and low-conduction information needed for the declared
$sp^3s^{\ast}$ reduction, indirect gap, conduction valley, and electron masses.
The exact rank, energy windows, excluded states, and interpolation domain remain
outputs of the Stage~03 target-subspace task.

A target subspace specification must say which parent calculation it consumes,
which $\mathbf k$ grid it uses, how states are selected or optimized, and over
which energies and reciprocal-space region fidelity is required. A visually
plausible band plot does not uniquely define this object.

\section{Projection and disentanglement}

Initial orbital projections provide trial directions from which a retained
frame may be constructed. Projection is an overlap operation between trial
orbitals and parent states; it is not by itself a proof that the desired
subspace has been selected.

When the desired bands are entangled with other states, disentanglement selects
an $M$-dimensional subspace from a larger outer space, often subject to inner
and outer energy-window constraints. This changes the selected subspace and is
therefore conceptually distinct from a unitary rotation within an already fixed
$M$-dimensional subspace. The following operations must remain separate:

\begin{description}
  \item[Projection.] Measures or constructs overlap with specified trial
    functions.
  \item[Disentanglement.] Selects a retained subspace from a larger candidate
    space.
  \item[Gauge transformation.] Changes the orthonormal frame within the same
    retained subspace.
  \item[Localization.] Chooses a frame according to a real-space localization
    objective.
  \item[Truncation.] Discards selected matrix elements or real-space blocks
    after representation.
\end{description}

Only the last of these directly removes represented couplings. Wannierization
alone is therefore not described as a low-rank approximation.

\section{Wannier transformation}

Let
\begin{equation}
  \{\lvert\psi_{m\mathbf k}\rangle\}_{m=1}^{M}
\end{equation}
be an orthonormal Bloch frame spanning the retained subspace on a uniform grid
of $N_{\mathbf k}$ points. A corresponding set of Wannier functions is
\begin{equation}
  \lvert w_{n\mathbf R}\rangle
  =
  \frac{1}{\sqrt{N_{\mathbf k}}}
  \sum_{\mathbf k}e^{-i\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf R}
  \sum_{m=1}^{M}
  \lvert\psi_{m\mathbf k}\rangle U_{mn}(\mathbf k),
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf R$ is a Bravais-lattice vector and
$U(\mathbf k)\in U(M)$ specifies the selected frame.

For a single isolated band, $U(\mathbf k)$ reduces to a momentum-dependent
phase. For a composite subspace it mixes the retained states. Replacing
$U(\mathbf k)$ by another unitary frame changes the individual Wannier
functions while preserving the subspace projector. The Wannier basis is
therefore gauge dependent even though the retained subspace is gauge invariant.

\section{Localization}

Spatial localization depends on the regularity of the selected Bloch frame over
the Brillouin zone. Maximally localized Wannier functions choose a gauge by
minimizing a declared spread functional. The spread and its decomposition are
diagnostics of that construction, not standalone measures of operator fidelity.
A small spread does not guarantee correct band interpolation, correct orbital
character, symmetry compatibility, or alignment with a separately generated
basis.

The construction record must retain at least the initial projections,
disentanglement windows when applicable, localization settings, convergence
history, Wannier centers, spreads, and software identities. Multiple candidate
constructions should consume the same accepted QE--Wannier interface artifacts
when they are intended to isolate sensitivity to projection or localization
choices.

\section{Reciprocal- and real-space Hamiltonians}

In the selected Bloch frame, let $H^{(\mathrm W)}(\mathbf k)$ denote the
retained Hamiltonian matrix. Its real-space blocks are
\begin{equation}
  H^{(\mathrm W)}_{mn}(\mathbf R)
  =
  \frac{1}{N_{\mathbf k}}
  \sum_{\mathbf k}e^{-i\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf R}
  H^{(\mathrm W)}_{mn}(\mathbf k),
\end{equation}
subject to the precise Fourier and degeneracy conventions used by the producing
software. The inverse transform reconstructs an interpolated reciprocal-space
matrix,
\begin{equation}
  H^{(\mathrm W)}(\mathbf k)
  =
  \sum_{\mathbf R}e^{i\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf R}
  H^{(\mathrm W)}(\mathbf R),
\end{equation}
when all represented blocks and the matching convention are retained.

Real-space decay can be diagnosed with quantities such as
$\|H^{(\mathrm W)}(\mathbf R)\|_{\mathrm F}$. Discarding distant blocks is a
separate approximation whose cutoff, boundary convention, and induced spectral
and operator residuals must be reported. The existence of localized functions
does not authorize an unrecorded hopping truncation.

\section{Validation of a candidate Wannier representation}

Stage~03 declares four complementary diagnostic families:

\begin{itemize}
  \item interpolation error over a declared domain, including withheld points;
  \item band-edge errors for the bulk quantities used downstream;
  \item Wannier centers and spreads, including unresolved symmetry or
    localization pathologies; and
  \item decay of the real-space Hamiltonian blocks.
\end{itemize}

These diagnostics answer different questions. Interpolation tests whether the
represented subspace reproduces selected parent eigenvalues. Centers and spreads
characterize the chosen localized frame. Real-space decay informs later
truncation choices. None alone establishes a physically aligned basis for
subtracting an independently generated operator.

An accepted bulk Wannier operator requires a reproducible subspace,
gauge-construction procedure, interpolation domain, and explicit truncation
status. No such accepted operator is asserted in this chapter.

\section{Why independent constructions require alignment}

Pristine and doped calculations, or two independent pristine constructions, may
produce localized orbitals with different phases, orderings, centers, mixtures,
and subspaces. Even identical software settings do not force identical gauges.
Directly subtracting their raw matrices can therefore measure coordinate choice
rather than a physical perturbation.

Before an identification map is formed, the comparison records must establish
compatible

\begin{itemize}
  \item cell and supercell geometry;
  \item atomic-site and lattice-translation mapping;
  \item orbital identities and ordering;
  \item retained dimensions and spin conventions;
  \item units and Fourier conventions; and
  \item scalar energy references.
\end{itemize}

For doped systems, the matched pristine comparison must use the same underlying
supercell convention. Structural relaxation is part of the physical impurity
branch, but it also makes the geometry map nontrivial and therefore explicit.

\section{Subspace diagnostics}

Let $B_{\mathrm r}$ and $B_{\mathrm c}$ contain orthonormal basis vectors for
reference and candidate spaces in a common ambient representation. Their
overlap matrix is
\begin{equation}
  S=B_{\mathrm r}^{\dagger}B_{\mathrm c}.
\end{equation}
The singular values $\sigma_j$ of $S$ define principal angles
\begin{equation}
  \theta_j=\arccos\sigma_j,
\end{equation}
within the applicable numerical range. These quantities diagnose subspace
agreement before coordinate alignment. Rank loss or large principal angles
cannot be repaired merely by relabelling orbitals.

When the spaces are sufficiently compatible, a polar or orthogonal-Procrustes
construction can supply a unitary coordinate identification. The exact map,
its direction, numerical conditioning, and residual diagnostics must be stored.
This method is a planned Stage~05 operation, not an alignment result already
obtained by the project.

\section{Transport and comparison}

For an alignment $A:\mathcal S_{\mathrm r}\rightarrow\mathcal S_{\mathrm c}$,
the candidate operator is transported to the reference space as
\begin{equation}
  H_{\mathrm c\rightarrow r}=A^{\dagger}H_{\mathrm c}A.
\end{equation}
Only after energy-reference compatibility and the remaining metadata checks may
a signed difference be formed. The difference convention must identify which
operator is candidate, reference, pristine, or doped rather than relying on an
ambiguous symbol $\Delta H$.

The alignment protocol is to be tested first on synthetic, controlled gauge
transformations where the expected map and transformed operator are known.
First-principles sensitivity tests then vary windows, projections, and
localization choices while preserving the relevant comparison controls. Exact
recovery in a synthetic test verifies software and mathematics; it does not by
itself validate a physical alignment between independent calculations.

\section{Energy and gauge sensitivity}

Alignment uncertainty is not exhausted by the unitary map. The eventual record
must report principal angles, overlap residuals, gauge-equivariance error, and
sensitivity to the construction choices used to produce both spaces. The
operational scalar energy-alignment estimator and its uncertainty must also be
frozen before interpreting a pristine--doped difference as an impurity
operator.

If rank compatibility, geometry correspondence, spin convention, energy zero,
or gauge validity remains unresolved, the comparison stops. Reporting that
boundary is preferable to constructing a numerically convenient subtraction
whose physical meaning is unidentified.
